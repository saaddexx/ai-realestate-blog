AI can speed up the planning of a neighborhood page. It cannot prove that buyers in a specific market are asking a given question, and it cannot confirm that a local fact is accurate. This post describes a workflow that keeps those two jobs separate: AI suggests and organizes, and you verify and decide.

**A note on evidence.** This workflow comes from a pilot research exercise on Capitol Hill, Seattle, not from a controlled test. The pilot produced four candidate page sections (neighborhood fit, transportation, parks and culture, housing types). It did not produce search-volume data, client inquiries, or evidence of what buyers actually search. Treat everything below as a method to try, not proven results.

## 1. Define the page before asking AI for ideas

Write down the scope first:

- **Client:** a residential real estate brokerage
- **Page reader:** a prospective homebuyer
- **Page purpose:** help buyers understand the neighborhood and find the brokerage's listings or services
- **Geography:** the boundary the brokerage actually uses

Neighborhood names often have no single official boundary, so record the definition you chose and confirm it matches the client's service area.

## 2. Label every question by where it came from

Collect questions from client call notes, emails to the brokerage, live search features (such as People Also Ask and autocomplete), and keyword-tool exports. You can also ask AI to brainstorm, but tag each item:

- **Observed:** seen in a live search result
- **Client-provided:** from a call, email, or other client material
- **AI-generated:** suggested by the model, not yet validated

"How do I get downtown from Capitol Hill?" is a good *candidate* question. It becomes an observed finding only after you capture where you saw that wording.

## 3. Group questions by what the page needs to do

- **Informational:** transit, parks, housing types
- **Commercial research:** comparing areas or property types
- **Transactional:** looking for current listings or contacting an agent

Then decide whether each question belongs on the neighborhood page, a separate guide, or the listings page. Topic groups are hypotheses until you confirm priority with real client inquiries and site data.

## 4. Verify each local fact at its source

Open the source yourself and check that it supports the exact claim:

- **Transit:** the transit agency's own station and service-alert pages
- **Parks and planning:** the city
- **Events:** the organizer
- **Prices and market figures:** MLS or brokerage data

Record the URL and the date you checked. Re-check close to publication, because schedules and alerts change. Don't include prices, commute times, school claims, or housing-stock percentages without a source for that exact claim and area. Also check the geography of any report: a study of a combined area is not automatically evidence about one neighborhood.

## 5. Turn it into a brief

A writer-ready brief includes the page purpose, the boundary used, each question with its source label, topic groups and placement, verified facts with links and dates, and a list of open items that still need client, MLS, or local-authority confirmation.

## Keep it useful, not just local-looking

Swapping one neighborhood name for another across many near-identical pages is the kind of thin content search engines discourage. Each page should carry genuinely local information that helps its reader.

## What this workflow does not tell you

It does not show that AI suggestions match real local demand, and it makes no promise about rankings, leads, or citations in AI search. Those are open questions.

**Bottom line:** use AI to generate and organize candidate topics, not to certify them. Your value is in checking where each idea came from and verifying what goes on the page.
